2.4 Complex Matrices and Forms

The three classes of matrices in Symmetric, Skew-Symmetric, and Orthogonal Matrices have complex counterparts which are of practical interest in certain applications, for instance, in quantum mechanics. This is mainly because of their spectra as shown in Theorem 1 in this section. The second topic is about extending quadratic forms of Eigenbases. Diagonalization. Quadratic Forms to complex numbers.

¶Description: 2.12: Hermitian, Skew-Hermitian, and Unitary Matrices

A square matrix A = [aki] is called

Hermitian if A¯T = A that is,  a¯kj = ajk skew-Hermitianif A¯T = −A, that is, a¯kj = −ajk unitary if A¯T = A−1. (2.82)

The first two classes are named after Charles Hermite. [61] ¶[61]Charles Hermite (1822 - 1901) was a French mathematician who studied analysis, number theory, and algebra. One of his most remarkable achievements was the proof of the transcendence of the number e.

From the definitions we see the following properties. If A is Hermitian, the entries on the main diagonal must satisfy ājj = ajj; that is, they are real. Similarly, if A is skew-Hermitian, then a¯jj = −ajj.

If we set ajj = α + iβ, this becomes α − iβ = −(α + iβ). Hence α = 0, so that ajj must be pure imaginary or 0.

are Hermitian, skew-Hermitian, and unitary matrices, respectively, as you may verify by using the definitions ■

If a Hermitian matrix is real, then A¯T = AT = A. Hence a real Hermitian matrix is a symmetric matrix (see Symmetric, Skew-Symmetric, and Orthogonal Matrices). Similarly, if a skew-Hermitian matrix is real, then A¯T = AT = −A. Hence a real skew-Hermitian matrix is a skew-symmetric matrix. Finally, if a unitary matrix is real, then A¯T = AT = AT. Hence a real unitary matrix is an orthogonal matrix.

This shows that Hermitian, skew-Hermitian, and unitary matrices generalize symmetric, skew-symmetric, and orthogonal matrices, respectively.

2.4.1 Eigenvalues

It is quite remarkable that the matrices under consideration have spectra [62] This means it has a set of eigenvalues. which can be characterized in a general way as follows:

¶ Theory 2.32: Eigenvalues

  • The eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix, and thus of a symmetric matrix, are real.

  • The eigenvalues of a skew-Hermitian matrix, and thus of a skew-symmetric matrix, are pure imaginary or zero.

  • The eigenvalues of a unitary matrix, and thus of an orthogonal matrix, have absolute value 1.

Key properties of orthogonal matrices [63] which are invariance of the inner product, and the orthonormality of rows and columns. generalize to unitary matrices in a remarkable way.

To see this, instead of Rn we now use the complex vector space Cn of all complex vectors with n complex numbers as components, and complex numbers as scalars. For such complex vectors the inner product is defined by:

a · b = a¯Tb, (2.84)

where the length or norm of such a complex vector is a real number defined by

A unitary transformation, that is, y = Ax with a unitary matrix A, preserves the value of the inner product Eq. (2.84), and therefore also the norm Eq. (2.85).
