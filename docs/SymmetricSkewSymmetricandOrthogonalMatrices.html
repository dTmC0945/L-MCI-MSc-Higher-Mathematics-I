2.2 Symmetric, Skew-Symmetric, and Orthogonal Matrices

We will now consider three (3) classes of real square matrices that, because of their remarkable properties, occur quite frequently in applications.

The first two matrices have already been mentioned in Linear Algebra I - Fundamentals. However, here the goal of current section is to show their remarkable properties.

¶ Description: 2.9: Symmetric, Skew-Symmetric, and Orthogonal Matrices

A real square matrix A = [aik] is called symmetric if transposition leaves it unchanged,

A T = A,thereforeakj = ajk, (2.42)

skew-symmetric if transposition gives the negative of A,

A T = −A,thereforeakj = −ajk, (2.43)

orthogonal if transposition gives the inverse of A,

A T = A−1. (2.44)

¶ Example 2.39: Symmetric, Skew-Symmetric, and Orthogonal Matrices

The matrices

[ −3 1 5 1 0 −2 5 −2 4 ], [ 0 9 −12 −9 0 20 12 −20 0 ], [ 2 3 1 3 2 3 −2 3 2 3 1 3 1 3 2 3 −2 3 ] (2.45)

are symmetric, skew-symmetric, and orthogonal, respectively ■

Every skew-symmetric matrix has all main diagonal entries zero.

Any real square matrix A may be written as the sum of a symmetric matrix R and a skew-symmetric matrix S, where

R = 1 2(A + AT)andS = 1 2(A − AT). (2.46)

¶ Theory 2.22: Eigenvalues of Symmetric and Skew-Symmetric Matrices

  • The eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix are real.

  • The eigenvalues of a skew-symmetric matrix are pure imaginary or zero.

2.2.1 Orthogonal Transformations and Orthogonal Matrices

Orthogonal transformations are transformations of the form where:

y = Axwhere A is an orthogonal matrix. (2.49)

With each vector x in ℝn such a transformation assigns a vector y in ℝn. For instance, the plane rotation through an angle 𝜃

y = [ y1 y2 ] = [ cos⁡𝜃 − sin⁡𝜃 sin⁡ 𝜃 cos⁡ 𝜃 ] [ x1 x2 ] (2.50)

is an orthogonal transformation. It can be shown that any orthogonal transformation in the plane or in three-dimensional space is a rotation. [55] possibly combined with a reflection in a straight line or a plane, respectively.

The main reason for the importance of orthogonal matrices is shown in the following theory.

¶ Theory 2.23: Invariance of Inner Product

An orthogonal transformation preserves the value of the inner product of vectors a and b in ℝn, defined by

a ·b = aTb = [a1⋯an] [ b1 ⋮ b n ] . (2.51)

Whereby, for any a and b in ℝn, orthogonal n × n matrix A, and u = Aa,v = Ab we have u · v = a · b.

Hence the transformation also preserves the length or norm of any vector a in Rn given by

‖a‖ = a · a = a T a. (2.52)

Orthogonal matrices have additional interesting properties as shown below.

¶ Theory 2.24: Orthonormality of Column and Row Vectors

A real square matrix is orthogonal if and only if its column vectors a1,…,anin addition to its row vectors. [56] form an orthonormal system, that is,

¶ Theory 2.25: Determinant of an Orthogonal Matrix

The determinant of an orthogonal matrix has the value + 1 or − 1.

¶ Theory 2.26: Eigenvalues of an Orthogonal Matrix

The eigenvalues of an orthogonal matrix A are real or complex conjugates in pairs and have absolute value 1.
