2.1 Determining Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

We will start simple, and consider multiplying nonzero vectors by a given square matrix, such as,

[ 6 3 4 7 ] [ 5 1 ] = [ 33 27 ], [ 6 3 4 7 ] [ 3 4 ] = [ 30 40 ]. (2.2)

We want to see what influence the multiplication of the given matrix has on the vectors.

In the first case, we get a totally new vector with a different direction and different length when compared to the original vector.

This is what usually happens and is of no interest here.

In the second case something interesting happens.

The multiplication produces a vector [ 30 40 ]T = 10 [ 3 4 ]T, which means the new vector has the same direction as the original vector.

The scale constant, which we denote by λ is 10. The problem of systematically finding such λ’s and nonzero vectors for a given square matrix will be the primary focus of this second chapter on Linear Algebra.

It is called the matrix eigenvalue problem or, more commonly, the eigenvalue problem.

Now we have the necessary background to formalize our observation. Let A = [ajk] be a given nonzero square matrix of dimension n × n.

Consider the following vector equation:

A x = λx (2.3)

The problem of finding nonzero x’s and λ’s that satisfy equation Eq. (2.56) is called an eigenvalue problem.

¶ Information 2.5: In a Nutshell

In the aforementioned explanation, A is a given square matrix, x is an unknown vector, and λ is an unknown scalar. Our task here is to find λ’s and nonzero x’s which satisfy Eq. (2.56). Geometrically, we are looking for vectors, x, for which the multiplication by A has the same effect as the multiplication by a scalar λ. [43] In other words, Ax should be proportional to x. Therefore, the multiplication has the effect of producing, from the original vector x, a new vector λx that has the same or opposite, minus sign, direction as the original vector.

You might be wondering why we require x to be nonzero. Given x = 0 is always a solution of Eq. (2.56) for any value of λ, because A0 = 0, it is of no interest and considered trivial.

To complete this introduction to the eigenvalues, let’s define some more terminology.

A value of λ, for which Eq. (2.56) has a solution x ≠ 0, is called an eigenvalue or characteristic value of the matrix A. [44] Another term for λ is a latent root [†-1] Sylvester, J. J. XXXIX. On the equation to the secular inequalities in the planetary theory. The London, Edinburgh, and Dublin Philosophical Magazine and Journal of Science 16, 267–269 (1883). This term was used to express that eigenvalues are inherently concealed within the matrix’s determinant structure. The corresponding solutions x ≠ 0 of Eq. (2.56) are called the eigenvectors or characteristic vectors of A corresponding to that eigenvalue λ.

The set of all the eigenvalues of A is called the spectrum of A [†-2] Nering, E. D. et al. Linear algebra and matrix theory (1970).

¶ Information 2.6: Usefulness of Spectrum

We will see, the spectrum consists of at least one eigenvalue and at most of n numerically different eigenvalues. The spectrum provides valuable information about a matrix.

In many applications, such as PageRank [†-3] Sullivan, D. What is google pagerank? A guide for searchers & webmasters. Search engine land (2007), one is interested in the dominant eigenvalue. [45] Which means it is the largest in absolute value which can be found by looking at the spectrum of a matrix.

Finally, the largest of the absolute values of the eigenvalues of A is called the spectral radius of A [†-4] Grandshteyn, I. & Ryzhik, I. Table of integrals, series, and products. New York: Academic Press 8, 3–3 (1980).

2.1.1 How to Find Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Now, with the new terminology for Eq. (2.56) we have in our disposal, we can say that the problem of determining the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a matrix is called an eigenvalue problem. [46] However, more precisely, we are considering an algebraic eigenvalue problem, as opposed to an eigenvalue problem involving ODEOrdinary Differential EquationOrdinary Differential Equations or PDEPartial Differential EquationPartial Differential Equations.

As mentioned in the introduction, eigenvalues have a very large number of applications in diverse fields such as in engineering, geometry, physics, mathematics, biology, environmental science, economics, psychology, and other areas. [47] Throughout this chapter we will encounter applications for elastic membranes, Markov processes, population models, and others.

Given, from the viewpoint of engineering applications, eigenvalue problems are the most important problems in connection with matrices and therefore should be closely studied.

Here A − λI is called the characteristic matrix and D(λ) the characteristic determinant of A. Eq. (2.60) is called the characteristic equation of A. By developing D(λ) we obtain a polynomial of nth degree in λ.

This is called the characteristic polynomial of A.

This proves the following important theorem.

¶ Theory 2.19: Eigenvalues

The eigenvalues of a square matrix A are the roots of the characteristic equation Eq. (2.60) of A. Therefore an n × n matrix has at least one eigenvalue and at most n numerically different eigenvalues. [48] For larger n, the actual computation of eigenvalues will, in general, require the use of Newton’s method or another numeric approximation method.

¶ Information 2.7: The Eigenvalues Needs to be First Determined

Once these are known, corresponding eigenvectors are obtained from the system Eq. (2.5), for instance, by the Gauss elimination, where λ is the eigenvalue for which an eigenvector is wanted. This is what we did in Example 1 and shall do again in the examples below.

Eigenvectors have the following properties.

¶ Theory 2.20: Eigenvectors and Eigenspace

If w and x are eigenvectors of a matrix A corresponding to the same eigenvalue λ, so are w + x [49] provided x ≠ −w. and kx for any k ≠ 0.

Therefore, the eigenvectors corresponding to one and the same eigenvalue λ of A, together with 0, form a vector space called the eigenspace of A corresponding to that λ.

In particular,

an eigenvector x is determined only up to a constant factor.

Therefore, we can normalize x, that is, multiply it by a scalar to get a unit vector.

For example, x1 = [ 1 2 ]Tin Example 1 has the length ‖x1‖ = 12 + 22 = 5. Therefore [1/ 52/ 5]T is a normalized eigenvector. [50] Also known as a unit eigenvector.

[52] It basically refers to the number of times a specific eigenvalue is repeated as a root of a matrix’s characteristic polynomial.
